Baku, Azerbaijan — Complete City Profile (2026)

1. City Population

Approximately 2.35–2.39 million in the city/metro area as of 2026 (estimate). Baku's metropolitan area population is estimated at 2,389,227, comprising about 22.85% of Azerbaijan's total population. This is a demographic estimate derived from UN/State Statistical Committee data, not a fresh census figure — treat as a current estimate, not an exact count. World Population Review

2. Nickname

"City of Winds" (Bādkube — Persian for "wind-pounded city"), reflecting the strong northern (Khazri) and southern (Gilavar) winds that regularly sweep the city.

3. Overview

Baku is the capital and largest city of Azerbaijan, the largest city on the Caspian Sea, and the largest city in the Caucasus region. It combines the medieval walled Old City with a modern skyline of oil-wealth architecture (Flame Towers, Heydar Aliyev Center), and functions as Azerbaijan's political, economic, scientific, and cultural capital.

4. History

Baku's roots trace to ancient settlement, with some sources citing Bronze/Iron Age evidence on the Absheron Peninsula. The first reliable written mentions of Baku appear in 5th-century sources, when the Byzantine historian Priscus of Panium described a "flame bursting from the sea rock" near the city. Arab geographers from the 9th–10th centuries described it as a small but important port city within the Shirvanshah state. After a devastating 1191 earthquake in Shamakhi, Shirvanshah Akhsitan I moved the state capital to Baku, sparking its growth; the 12th–15th centuries saw construction of the Shirvanshahs' Palace complex within the Icherisheher fortress. In 1501 the city became part of the Safavid state, and in 1806 Baku was incorporated into the Russian Empire, beginning its modern industrial era. The 19th-century oil boom transformed Baku into a major global petroleum center, and it later became capital of the Azerbaijan Democratic Republic (1918) and then the Azerbaijan SSR, before becoming capital of independent Azerbaijan in 1991. 5. Founded

Not available as an exact founding date — historians agree the precise founding date is unknown. Archaeological materials on the Absheron peninsula date to the 3rd–1st centuries BC, but the exact date on which Baku was founded is not known exactly. The city is first reliably attested in 5th-century written sources; some Azerbaijani government sources place continuous settlement in the area at roughly 5,500 years. Preslib

6. Historical Significance

Baku was capital of the Shirvanshah state (from the 12th century) and later the short-lived Baku Khanate; it became a global center of the oil industry from the 1870s onward (birthplace of the modern oil industry, home to the world's first industrially drilled oil wells) and briefly capital of the independent Azerbaijan Democratic Republic (1918–1920), the first secular democratic republic in the Muslim world. Its Old City (Icherisheher) is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

9. Rivers

Baku has no major river running through it. The city sits on the Caspian Sea coast (Bay of Baku) and is instead characterized by salt lakes (e.g., Boyukshor, Khodasan, Bulbula) and mud volcanoes on the surrounding Absheron Peninsula rather than a river system.

10. Climate

Semi-arid/Mediterranean-influenced climate (Köppen: Csa/BSk borderline) — hot, dry summers and mild, occasionally wet winters, with the port remaining ice-free year-round. Baku sits at roughly the same latitude as Greece and Italy and generally has a Mediterranean-type climate, though it is strongly shaped by frequent strong winds (the Khazri from the north and Gilavar from the south). 12. UNESCO Sites

Walled City of Baku with the Shirvanshah's Palace and Maiden Tower (Icherisheher) — inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2000. Built on a site inhabited since the Palaeolithic period, it reveals evidence of Zoroastrian, Sasanian, Arabic, Persian, Shirvani, Ottoman, and Russian presence in cultural continuity, and includes the 12th-century Maiden Tower (built over structures dating to the 7th–6th centuries BC) and the 15th-century Shirvanshahs' Palace. The site was placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2003 and was removed from that endangered list in 2009 following conservation efforts. UNESCOUNESCO

3. Overview

Fuzuli is a city in southwestern Azerbaijan, capital of Fuzuli District, located in the Karabakh economic region near the foothills of the Lesser Caucasus. Named after the celebrated 16th-century Azerbaijani/Ottoman poet Muhammad Fuzuli, the city was occupied by Armenian forces from 1993 to 2020 and left largely in ruins. Since its recapture by Azerbaijan in October 2020, Fuzuli has become a centerpiece of the government's Karabakh reconstruction program, including a new international airport and planned "smart city" infrastructure.

4. History

The Fuzuli district was originally established in 1930 based on the settlement of Garabulag and was named Garyagin (Karyagin). In 1959, on the occasion of the 400th anniversary of the poet Muhammad Fuzuli, the district and town were renamed Fuzuli. On August 23, 1993, Armenian armed forces attacked and occupied Fuzuli city along with 58 surrounding villages during the First Nagorno-Karabakh War, expelling the local Azerbaijani population and turning the city into a ghost town. The occupation resulted in more than 1,100 residents of the wider region being killed, 113 taken hostage, and roughly 1,450 left disabled, with extensive destruction of homes and historical/religious monuments. On October 17, 2020, Azerbaijani forces recaptured the city during the 2020 Nagorno-Karabakh War, after which the Azerbaijani government began a large-scale demining and reconstruction campaign. News.az + 5

5. Founded

Fuzuli was founded in 1827, according to historical city records, though the district itself was formally established in 1930 under the earlier name Garyagin. News.az

6. Historical Significance

Fuzuli holds cultural significance as the namesake city of Muhammad Fuzuli, one of the most important poets in Azerbaijani, Ottoman, and broader Turkic literary history, renowned for pairing his poetry with mugham (traditional Azerbaijani classical music). The district is also historically significant for the nearby Azykh Cave — the site of a Neanderthal-era jawbone discovery in 1968, estimated to be over 300,000 years old and considered one of the oldest proto-human remains found in the region — and for a concentration of historic mausoleums and caravanserais, including the 1681 Garghabazar Caravanserai and the 1683 Haji Giyaseddin Mosque, many of which were damaged or destroyed during the occupation. Since 2020, Fuzuli has become symbolically significant as a flagship site of Azerbaijan's post-war reconstruction and resettlement program in the Karabakh region. AzerNews

3. Overview

Gabala (also spelled Qabala) is a city in northern Azerbaijan, capital of Gabala District, situated in the foothills of the Greater Caucasus mountains roughly 225 km from Baku. 4. History

Ancient Qabala city was the capital of Caucasian Albania for roughly 900 years, from the 4th century BC until the 5th century AD, and its great fortified walls remain today at the site now known as "Chukhur Gabala," located about 20 km southwest of the modern town center. Over subsequent centuries the city became part of the state of the Shirvanshahs and later the Sheki Khanate. During the Soviet era the town was known as Gutgashinli (Qutqashen), named in honor of Ismayil bey Gutgashinli (1806–1861), a decorated military general and writer. Following Azerbaijan's independence in 1991, the city and district officially reclaimed the historical name Gabala. The post-independence era saw major tourism development: the Gabala Music Festival was founded in 2009, Gabala International Airport opened in 2011, the city was awarded "Cultural Capital of the CIS" status in 2013, and the Tufandag ski complex opened in 2014 — transformations that turned Gabala into a year-round international tourism hub. 3. Overview

Ganja is Azerbaijan's second-largest city, located at the north-eastern foot of the Lesser Caucasus Mountains, roughly 375 km west of Baku, situated between the capital and Tbilisi, Georgia. It is one of the country's oldest and most culturally significant cities, renowned as the birthplace of the celebrated 12th-century poet Nizami Ganjavi and as the first (temporary) capital of the Azerbaijan Democratic Republic in 1918.

4. History

The city was founded circa 859 by the Arab governor Muhammad ibn Khalid. During the 12th–13th centuries, Ganja flourished as the second capital of the Eldiguzid state and became home to the great poet Nizami Ganjavi. In 1235 the city was captured and devastated by Mongol troops. In 1606 Ganja became part of the Safavid Empire under Shah Abbas I, who relocated and actively restored the city. By 1747, following the weakening of Persian central power, Ganja became the center of the semi-independent Ganja Khanate. In 1804 the city was incorporated into the Russian Empire and renamed Elisabethpol, in honor of the spouse of Emperor Alexander I. In 1918 Ganja briefly served as the first capital of the newly proclaimed Azerbaijan Democratic Republic. During the Soviet period, in 1935, the city was renamed Kirovabad and developed as an industrial center, before reclaiming its historic name Ganja in 1989. More recently, Ganja was named European Youth Capital in 2016 — the first EYC host outside the European Union — and became Cultural Capital of the Commonwealth of Independent States for 2017. 3. Overview

Lachin is a town in southwestern Azerbaijan and the administrative center of Lachin District, part of the East Zangezur economic region. It is located within the strategically vital Lachin corridor, historically the key overland route linking the Nagorno-Karabakh region with Armenia. 4. History

Lachin District was established on August 8, 1930. The district was occupied by Armenian armed forces on May 18, 1992, during the First Nagorno-Karabakh War, resulting in the expulsion of its predominantly Azerbaijani and Kurdish inhabitants and widespread destruction of infrastructure and cultural sites. 2. Nickname

Lankaran is popularly described in tourism materials as Azerbaijan's "Subtropical Pearl," reflecting its distinctive humid subtropical climate and lush vegetation, unusual for the country as a whole.

3. Overview

Lankaran is a city in southeastern Azerbaijan on the Caspian Sea coast, near the southern border with Iran, and serves as the administrative center of the Lankaran-Astara Economic Region. Backed by the Talysh Mountains, it is known for its humid subtropical climate, tea and citrus cultivation, distinct Talysh cultural heritage, and status as former capital of the historic Talysh Khanate.

4. History

Lankaran was first mentioned in the 17th century and became capital of the Talysh khanate of Iran in the 18th century. The khanate was under the rule of the Iranian Zand and Qajar families until 1828. Russia briefly took control of Lankaran during the Russo-Persian War of 1722–1723, but the city was returned to Iran under the 1732 Treaty of Resht. During the Russo-Persian War of 1804–1813, Russian general Pyotr Kotlyarevsky captured Lankaran's fortress, and the 1813 Treaty of Gulistan formally ceded the city to Russia. Iran attempted to retake the city during the Russo-Persian War of 1826–1828, but was forced to relinquish it again under the 1828 Treaty of Turkmenchay, after which Lankaran remained under Russian, then Soviet, then independent Azerbaijani control. On October 31, 2021, Lankaran joined the UNESCO Creative Cities Network in the gastronomy category, reflecting its modern cultural and culinary significance. britannica + 5

Lankaran's principal historical significance rests on its role as capital of the independent Talysh Khanate during the 18th and early 19th centuries, making it a key seat of regional Talysh political power before its incorporation into the Russian Empire. 3. Overview

Nakhchivan is the capital and largest city of the Nakhchivan Autonomous Republic, a landlocked exclave of Azerbaijan separated from the rest of the country by Armenia. Located 450 km west of Baku, spread over the foothills of the Zangezur Mountains, the city is one of the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in the Caucasus and is renowned for its ancient mausoleums, distinctive medieval "Nakhchivan school" of architecture, and legendary associations with the biblical/Quranic figure of Noah. Wikipedia

4. History

Nakhchivan has an extremely long historical record, with archaeological evidence of settlement dating back millennia; the region has passed through Achaemenid, Sassanian, Arab Caliphate, Seljuk, Eldiguzid Atabeg, Mongol, Timurid, Safavid, and Qajar rule before Russian annexation. The region was part of Iran's Safavid Dynasty in the mid-to-late 16th century and was awarded to Imperial Russia in 1828 following the Russo-Persian War. In 1917 the region revolted against Imperial Russia and was placed under a provisional government; in May 1918, Nakhchivan was contested between the newly formed Azerbaijan Democratic Republic and Democratic Republic of Armenia, briefly occupied by the Ottoman Empire in June 1918 before a British occupation followed. The Bolsheviks seized the territory in 1920 and declared it an autonomous republic while maintaining close ties to Azerbaijan. Nakhchivan was known as the Nakhchivan Soviet Socialist Republic (1920–1923), the Nakhchivan Autonomous Region (1923–1924), and the Nakhchivan Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic (1924–1990) before becoming the Nakhchivan Autonomous Republic in 1990. In 1990, Nakhchivan declared independence from the Soviet Union in protest against the marginalization of nationalist movements; a year later, it reaffirmed its status as an autonomous region of Azerbaijan governed by a democratically elected legislature. WorldAtlas + 4

6. Historical Significance

Nakhchivan holds deep historical and religious significance as a claimed site associated with the biblical/Quranic prophet Noah — home to "Noah's Tomb," a mausoleum believed by local tradition to be his burial site. The city was also the seat of important medieval Islamic architecture, producing the renowned "Nakhchivan school" of design under 12th-century architect Ajami Nakhchivani. In 2018, Nakhchivan was named an Islamic Culture Capital, reflecting its status as a center of Islamic heritage. Zaqatala (Zagatala), Azerbaijan

1. 2. Nickname: Often referred to informally as Azerbaijan's "nut capital" or "hazelnut capital" due to its role as the country's leading hazelnut- and walnut-growing region.

3. Overview: Zaqatala is a city in northwestern Azerbaijan, administrative center of Zaqatala (Zagatala) District, in the Shaki-Zaqatala Economic Region on the southern slopes of the Greater Caucasus Mountains, near the border with Georgia and Russia's Dagestan region. The city is divided into two parts, Zaka and Tala, by the Tala (Katekh) River, and it's known for lush forests, mountain scenery, and a multi-ethnic population. Wikivoyage

4. History: The Zagatala district was once part of Caucasian Albania, later coming under Georgian/Kakhetian political and Orthodox Christian influence, and subsequently seeing Avar migration from Dagestan and Persian incursions under Shah Abbas. In 1844 the Ilisu Sultanate was absorbed into Russian administration, and that same year a fortress named "New Zakatala" was established; by 1851 the town of Zakatala became the fortress's outpost, and on April 5, 1860, Tsar Alexander II formally created the Zakatala District. The town's fortress, built in the 1830s by Russian forces during the Caucasian War, later gained fame as one of the prisons for the mutinous crew of the battleship Potemkin following the failed 1905 Revolution. Zaqatala was part of the Transcaucasian Democratic Federative Republic (1917–1918) and the Georgian Democratic Republic (1918–1921) before passing to Soviet Azerbaijan in March 1922. Uzgen + 4

5. Founded: The fortress and associated settlement of "New Zakatala" were established in 1844 (per detailed historical sources), though some tourism sources cite founding "in the 17th century by Zakeria." The district as a modern administrative unit was established August 8, 1930. Uzgen

6. Historical Significance: Historically part of Caucasian Albania and later the Saingilo cultural zone straddling Georgian and Azerbaijani influence; site of the Jar-Balakan (Jaro-Belokan) confederacy that resisted Russian imperial expansion; the Zaqatala Fortress played a strategic military role during the Caucasian War and the 1905 Revolution.

7. Area: Zagatala District covers 1,350 km² (520 sq mi) (official district figure). City-proper area is not separately published. Wikipedia

8. Elevation: 518 m (1,699 ft) — official/commonly cited figure (Wikipedia infobox); Wikivoyage separately cites an altitude of 600 m. Treat as an approximate/officially-sourced estimate given the discrepancy. WikipediaWikivoyage

9. Rivers: The Tala (Katekh) River runs through and divides the city.

10. Climate: Not an officially published Köppen classification specific to the city, but per travel-guide sources it has a humid subtropical mountain-influenced climate: rainy springs with much of the annual ~120 cm of precipitation falling then; summer temperatures of 30–35°C with verdant mountains; browning leaves and rain in fall; and winters with average snow depths of 50–60 cm in the mountains.
